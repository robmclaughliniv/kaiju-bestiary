- **Estimated length:** 67 m

## Morphology

Glassmother resembles a low-slung amphibious mammal whose body has become partially transparent through generations of symbiotic adaptation. The dorsal surface is not skin but a living greenhouse membrane — translucent, photosynthetic, and veined with nutrient channels that feed the garden within.

Through the membrane, observers see preserved plant communities: ferns, mosses, flowering species, and small trees that have not existed in the external Mourning Reach for centuries. Symbiotic organisms — insects, fungi, bacteria, and at least one vertebrate species — maintain this interior ecology.

The body is supported by calcified root-structures that function as bones. Long tusks curve downward for digging nutrient-rich sediments. The ventral surface is armored with compressed peat plates that neutralize toxins on contact.

## Ecology and behavior

Glassmother follows ancient migration routes through the Mourning Reach that predate the current poisoned ecosystem. These routes are now partially blocked by Venomvine growth, forcing the creature to navigate the vine maze with surprising precision. Whether it follows chemical signals, inherited memory, or some other guidance system remains disputed.

When threatened, Glassmother releases a cloud of neutralized spores from specialized dorsal chambers. These spores temporarily suppress Venomvine toxin production within a localized area, creating safe passage for other organisms. The effect lasts hours to days depending on wind and humidity.

The creature does not feed in the conventional sense. Its symbiotic garden produces nutrients through photosynthesis, supplemented by minerals extracted from filtered sediment.

## Recorded abilities

### Greenhouse Membrane
The translucent dorsal surface filters sunlight to interior plant communities while blocking harmful radiation and toxins.

### Spore Release
Emits neutralized spores that temporarily suppress Venomvine toxins and other chemical threats in a localized radius.

### Sediment Dredging
Uses tusks and ventral plates to stir and filter wetland sediment, extracting minerals and concentrating toxins into pearl-like growths.

### Symbiotic Cascade
The interior ecosystem can rapidly adapt to external changes, producing new compounds, organisms, or structural adaptations within weeks rather than generations.

## Guild advisory

Do not approach during spore release events. The neutralized spores are not toxic but may trigger allergic reactions and temporarily interfere with chemical sensors.

Do not attempt to harvest interior plant species without Guild authorization. Many are genetically distinct from external populations and may carry unrecognized pathogens.

Glassmother is not aggressive but will defend its route if blocked. Its mass and armored ventral surface can demolish structures and reshape waterways.

## Cultural record

Wetland communities call Glassmother the Garden That Walks, the Ferry, or the Last Green. Some settlements time their seasonal movements to its migration, following the safe corridors it creates. Others consider it a sacred trust and forbid harvesting from its back.

A minority of communities have developed ritualized "gardening" practices — pruning overgrowth, removing diseased plants, and planting seeds from their own stored collections into Glassmother's dorsal membrane. The Guild does not officially sanction this practice but has documented at least three cases where human-tended gardens produced novel hybrid species.

> From the Widowfen prayer-cycle: "Where the garden walks, we follow. Where the garden rests, we plant. Where the garden sleeps, we wait."

## Researcher's comment

> It is not merely preserving the old world. It is carrying it forward, one migration at a time. The question is not whether we should protect Glassmother. The question is whether we deserve what it preserves.

## Field guidance

- Mark migration routes but do not obstruct them.
- Record spore release timing and duration.
- Maintain distance during sediment dredging; turbidity can mask structural hazards.
- Report any novel plant species observed in the dorsal garden.

## Canon connections

- Venomvine networks partially block Glassmother's migration routes, creating a predator-prey dynamic without direct contact.
- Bloomwraith colonies feed on animals killed during Glassmother stampedes when safe corridors collapse.
- Orchidia has been observed standing motionless near Glassmother migration paths, possibly responding to spore chemical signals.
- The dorsal garden preserves plant species believed extinct in the external Mourning Reach ecosystem.

## Art direction

Low, broad silhouette with a translucent dorsal membrane showing internal plant communities. Emphasize the contrast between the poisoned external wetland (dark, muted colors) and the preserved interior garden (vibrant greens, flowers, light). Long curved tusks for sediment dredging. Calcified root-bones visible through translucent skin. Ventral armor plates of compressed peat. Include human observers for scale — perhaps a settlement built on its migration route or researchers in a small boat alongside.

Dominant tones: deep wetland brown, translucent amber-greens, preserved garden colors (bright greens, flower accents), calcified bone-white, peat black.

The creature should feel simultaneously ancient and fragile — a living museum carrying extinction forward.

## Open questions

- Is the symbiotic garden genetically stable, or does it drift over generations?
- Does Glassmother navigate Venomvine mazes through memory, chemical sensing, or some form of communication with the vine networks?
- Why do Orchidia sightings cluster near Glassmother migration routes?
- Can the preserved plant species survive outside Glassmother's body, or have they become entirely dependent on the symbiotic system?
- What caused the original poisoning of the Mourning Reach, and did Glassmother evolve before or after this event?
